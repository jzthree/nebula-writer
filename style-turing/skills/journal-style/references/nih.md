# NIH grant applications

Measured on the Project Summaries and Project Narratives of about 1,200 funded new NIH awards (fiscal years 2019–2024; R01, R21, R03, R35, K01, K08, K23, K99, F31 and F32), and read against funded applications that their authors shared openly. Numbers are averages across many paragraphs, not quotas. Funded applications are written by scientists under page limits and deadlines, not by professional editors: they argue for the work, and they read as dense, direct and somewhat uneven. Compared with machine-written grant text, funded text makes its case through specifics (the disease burden with its number, the preliminary finding, the model, the assay) rather than through adjectives and announcements, and it has fewer, plainer connectives.

## House conventions

- **Tense.** The proposed work is in the future tense: "will" is the most common verb (about 12 per 1,000 words in summaries, 11 in narratives). Background is in the present tense, and the applicants' own prior and preliminary work is in the past or present perfect ("We have shown", "Our preliminary data show", "I found").
- **Person follows the mechanism.** Research grants (R01, R21, R03, R35) use "we/our" (about 2 per 100 words). Fellowships (F31, F32) use "I" for the applicant's own work and "we" or "our lab" for the lab's prior work, often in the same summary. Career awards (K01, K08, K23, K99) often describe the candidate in the third person ("Dr. Smith is a …", "The candidate will …") and the research in "I" or "we". Keep whatever person the content uses; do not convert a summary to one person.
- **Aim labels.** Write them the way the content gives them: "Aim 1", "Specific Aim 1", "In Aim 1, we will", "(Aim 2)", "Aim 1." followed by the aim's title in sentence case. Inside a summary they are inline, not a list.
- **No citations** in the Project Summary or Narrative. Author-year citations, when the content has them (Specific Aims, Significance), stay as given.
- **Abbreviations** are defined at first use in parentheses and then used bare. Parentheses are common (about 12 per 1,000 words in summaries): abbreviations, aim pointers, short glosses.
- **Numbers.** Disease burden and cohort sizes in numerals with units or "%" ("more than 250 million cases annually", "n = 400 participants"). Keep them as given.
- **American spelling.**
- **Names and roles.** Keep the applicant's, mentor's and institution's names exactly as given ("Dr. Smith", "the Covert lab at Stanford University").

## Punctuation and sentence shape

- **Sentence length.** Summary sentences average about 26 words, with a wide spread (SD about 10 words): a 12-word statement of the hypothesis can sit next to a 45-word sentence carrying an aim and its methods. Narrative sentences are longer and more even (about 28–30 words).
- **Commas.** About 1.1–1.2 per sentence, fewer than in journal prose. Sentences are built from a subject, a verb and a long object or prepositional chain, not from stacked clauses.
- **Semicolons and colons** are rare (about 1–2 per 1,000 words each). The colon is used mostly after an aim label or in "(i) …; (ii) …" lists, which appear in some summaries. Do not introduce a sentence's payoff with a colon.
- **Dashes** are rare (about one in eight summaries). Use commas or parentheses.
- **Paragraphs.** Most summaries are one paragraph of about 270 words (10 sentences). Some split into two to four paragraphs (background, aims, impact). A narrative is one paragraph of about 85 words, almost always three sentences.

## Voice, connectives and vocabulary

- **Sentence-initial connectives** open about one sentence in twelve in summaries, so about one per summary, and are rarer in narratives. "However" is by far the most common (a real turn to the gap), then "Thus", "Therefore", "Furthermore", "In addition", "Finally". Do not open consecutive sentences with connectives, and do not use "Moreover", "Additionally" and "Importantly" more than once in a summary.
- **Common sentence openings** in summaries: "We will …", "In Aim 1, …", "The proposed …", "In this …", "I will …", "Dr. …", "This proposal/project …", "However, the …", "We have …", "We hypothesize …", "We propose …", "Based on …", "Our preliminary …". In narratives: "The proposed …", "This project/proposal/study/research …", "The goal …", "We will …", "Findings from …", "Understanding …". Openings that name the study ("This project", "The proposed research") are normal in grants, more than in papers, especially in narratives.
- **Evaluative words** are part of the genre and are used at a measured rate. Per 1,000 words of summary: novel 1.6, critical 0.9, key 0.8, important 0.7, innovative 0.5, significant 0.5, unique 0.5, major 0.5, elucidate 0.4, insights 0.3, fundamental 0.3, essential 0.3. A 270-word summary therefore carries about two or three of them in total, usually "novel" plus one other. Narratives use more per word ("novel" 3.1, "critical" 1.5 per 1,000), because they make the case in three sentences. Keep the ones the content has; add none.
- **Rare in funded text:** crucial, pivotal, leverage(s), robust, comprehensive, highlight(s), transformative, groundbreaking, cutting-edge, unprecedented, paradigm-shifting, holistic, multifaceted, foster, "shed light on", "pave the way". Each appears in well under one summary in five. Use one only if the content has it.
- **Hedges** are few in summaries (most have none), and "may", "could" or "potential" appear mostly in the final impact sentence or the narrative ("could lead to new treatments", "may provide clues").
- **Gap statements** are concrete and short: "the mechanisms are unknown", "no studies have examined …", "it is not clear whether …", "A major challenge in … is the lack of …". Do not inflate them ("a critical and largely unexplored gap in our understanding").
- **The applicants' own wording.** Keep their terms, model names, coined labels and occasional enthusiasm ("our exciting findings") as given. Funded text is not uniformly polished: a long sentence that carries the whole hypothesis, a repeated "will", an aim stated as a bare imperative ("Determine whether …") are all normal.

## Funded text is not copy-edited

Reviewers comparing drafts with funded applications point first to polish: the draft is smoother than anything the applicants wrote. Funded text was written by scientists against a deadline and never passed through a copy editor. Without introducing errors:

- **Keep the applicants' constructions,** including wordy or roundabout ones ("the candidate plans to address the following specific aims", "with the goal of being able to", "towards"), and their exact form of an abbreviation or label, however idiosyncratic ("assessment-EMA", "Aim1").
- **Do not tighten.** Do not merge two of their sentences into one, condense a clause into a modifier, or recast a plain, clunky phrase as a smooth participial ending (", leading to more efficient treatment", ", enabling …").
- **Do not tidy structure.** Do not turn a run of aims or goals into a neat parallel "(1) … and (2) …" or a matched series unless the content has it that way, and do not even out the lengths of the aim sentences.
- **Add nothing for clarity.** No parenthetical gloss, defining abbreviation, appositive or "to address this gap"-type link that the content lacks.
- **Let enthusiasm and loose words stand** where the content has them ("exciting", "very", "aligns well", "a lot of").

## Section rules

**Project Summary.** ~270 words, ~10 sentences of ~26 words, usually one paragraph. Its job is the whole application in brief, for reviewers and for the public record.
- The usual order is: the problem and its burden (one or two sentences, with a number if the content has one); what is known and the gap ("However, …"); the long-term goal, objective or central hypothesis; the preliminary data or the team's position, briefly; the aims, one or two sentences each ("In Aim 1, we will …" or "Aim 1 will determine …"); the expected outcome and impact in one closing sentence. Training awards add the candidate, the mentors and the training plan, often in the opening or closing sentences.
- Aims are stated with their approach (model, method, cohort) in the same sentence. Do not write aims as a list of bare goals followed by a separate methods sentence.
- Close with one concrete impact sentence ("These studies will identify …", "The results will provide …", "This work will establish …"). Do not add a second, general one.
- Do not open with "In recent years", "There is a growing need", or an announcement ("This proposal aims to address the critical need …").

**Project Narrative.** ~85 words, almost always three sentences, one paragraph, in plain language for the public: no aims, few numbers, no unexplained abbreviations.
- Typical structure: the health problem or open question (sentence 1); what this project will do, often opening "This project/The proposed research/This study will …" (sentence 2); how the results could improve health (sentence 3, "could lead to", "will inform", "may provide").
- Public-health relevance is stated once, concretely (the disease, the population, the kind of treatment or prevention).

**Specific Aims** (one page; not part of the measured corpus, so use the examples and these rules).
- Opening paragraph: the problem and its significance, what is known, the gap, then the long-term goal, the objective of this application, and the central hypothesis with what it is based on (often the preliminary data). Many funded pages use the explicit labels ("The long-term goal of our research is …", "The objective of this proposal is …", "Our central hypothesis is …"); others state the same moves without labels. Follow the content.
- Each aim: a bold one-line title as a full declarative or imperative sentence ("Aim 1: Determine the role of mTORC1 in adipose tissue lipid homeostasis."), then two to four sentences on the rationale or working hypothesis, the approach (model, method, comparison) and what it will determine. Sub-aims (1A, 1B) when the content has them.
- Closing paragraph: expected outcomes and impact, specific to the aims ("These studies will provide …"), sometimes the next step (the R01 that will follow).

**Significance, Innovation, Approach** (not measured; follow the content and the general rules).
- Significance and Innovation often use run-in headings ("Significance.", "Innovation.") and numbered points. Significance argues from the problem's importance and the specific gap; Innovation names what is new (concept, method, model) plainly, without stacking "novel".
- Approach subsections use run-in headings such as Rationale, Preliminary data, Experimental design, Expected outcomes, Potential problems and alternative strategies, Timeline. Write experimental design as what will be done, in what system, with what comparison and what measurement, and say how the result will be interpreted.
