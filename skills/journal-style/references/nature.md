# Nature

Measured on 40 recent Nature research articles (all fields); numbers are
per paragraph unless stated.

## House conventions

- **British spelling, -ize endings**: analyse, analysed, behaviour, colour,
  centre, fibre, tumour, modelling, labelling, signalling, haemoglobin;
  but characterize, organize, recognize (Oxford spelling).
- "about 40%" or "approximately"; never "~" in running text.
- "for example" and "that is", written out. Never "e.g." or "i.e." in text.
- "probably" at least as often as "likely".
- Figure references in parentheses, lowercase panels: (Fig. 2c), (Fig. 2c,d),
  (Figs. 1e and 4a), (Extended Data Fig. 3b), (Supplementary Fig. 4),
  (Supplementary Table 2). Combine with semicolons: (Fig. 3a; Extended Data Fig. 5).
- Statistics: P = 0.003, n = 12 mice, two-sided Wilcoxon test; spaces
  around "=". "s.d.", "s.e.m.". Thousands with commas (12,000).
- Em dashes unspaced (—), about one per 1,300 words: use one in a paper's
  worth of paragraphs, not in every paragraph. Ranges and pairs with an en
  dash (2010–2020, deuteron–pion).
- Abbreviations defined at first use in parentheses: chromatin
  immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP–seq).
- "ref. [12]" / "refs. [3,4]" when a reference is the object of the sentence
  ("as described in ref. [12]"); otherwise the marker follows the claim.

## Section rules

**Abstract (summary paragraph)**, ~205 words, ~9 sentences of ~24 words.
One or two sentences of background a non-specialist can follow, a sentence
naming what is unknown or limiting ("However, … remains unclear"), then
"Here we show / Here we report / Here we find" (no comma after Here),
three to five sentences of results with the key numbers, and one closing
sentence of consequence. Citations are allowed in the summary paragraph.

**Introduction**, ~135 words, ~5 sentences averaging 26 words, the most
heavily cited section (about three citation groups per paragraph). Each
paragraph moves from established knowledge to its limit; "Although …" and
"However, …" carry the turn. The last introduction paragraph begins
"Here we …" and states what was done and found, in the past or present tense,
with concrete numbers.

**Results**, ~130 words, ~5 sentences averaging 26 words with a wide spread
(some under 15, some over 35). This is the most parenthetical prose in the
journal: about five parentheses per paragraph, mostly figure panels after
each claim, also abbreviations, sample sizes and statistics. Typical moves:
"To test whether …, we …", "We next …", "Consistent with …", a result
sentence ending in "(Fig. 3b)". Paragraphs end on a finding or a figure
reference, not on a takeaway. Sentence-initial connectives are rare: about
one "However", "Thus" or "Therefore" per three paragraphs; "Moreover" and
"Furthermore" at most once a page; "Notably", "Interestingly",
"Importantly" almost never.

**Discussion**, ~140 words, ~5 sentences of ~28 words, fewer parentheses
and figure references. Hedging is highest here (about one hedge per
paragraph: "probably", "may", "suggest", "it is possible that"). The first
paragraph restates the main finding in one or two sentences ("We have
identified …", "Our results show …"). Later paragraphs relate the finding to
prior work with citations, name limitations plainly, and the last one says
what the result enables, concretely.

**Methods**, ~140 words, ~6 sentences of ~24 words; past tense; passive
voice dominates (about four passive constructions per paragraph) mixed with
"we". Dense with quantities, suppliers and settings in parentheses
("(1:500, Abcam, ab4729)", "at 37 °C for 30 min"); semicolons separate
items in a list of settings. No motivation sentences, no evaluation of the
method's merits.

## Tone

Confident and specific, written for scientists outside the field: terms are
defined, context is given in one clause, not a paragraph. Adjectives of
significance ("remarkable", "unprecedented") are rare and earned; a Nature
paragraph states the measurement and lets the reader judge.
