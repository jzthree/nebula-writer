# Science

Measured on Science research articles (training profile: 21 abstracts, 35
introduction, 274 Results, 69 Discussion and 236 Methods paragraphs).
Numbers are per paragraph unless stated. Expert readers compare skill-written
paragraphs with published ones. The commonest tells are text that is too
tidy, too even and too careful. Published Science prose has longer, looser
sentences, more evaluative words, fewer hedges and fewer semicolons than
machine drafts.

## House conventions

- **American spelling**: analyze, behavior, color, center, labeled, modeling,
  signaling, tumor.
- **Serial comma** before "and"/"or" in lists of three or more ("A, B, and C").
- Approximations with "~" (~40%, ~3 kb) or "approximately"; "about" is rare.
- "e.g.," and "i.e.," in parentheses; "for instance" in running text.
- Figure references use **capital panel letters**: (Fig. 2B), (Fig. 2, B and C),
  (Fig. 3, A to D). Supplementary items are lowercase: (fig. S4), (table S2),
  (movie S3). Combine main and supplementary items with a semicolon:
  (Fig. 4A; fig. S7). Put statistics in their own parenthesis next to the
  figure reference rather than merging both into one long parenthetical.
- Numeric ranges in text are usually written with an en dash (10–30%, 2–4 hours).
  "to" appears in figure-panel ranges. Write numbers in the form the notes give
  them; do not convert every range or number to one format.
- Statistics are compact: P < 0.01, (n = 8 mice). Thousands take commas.
- **Abbreviations**: give the full term first and the abbreviation in
  parentheses, at first use in the paper. A paragraph from the middle of
  Results, Discussion or Methods uses established abbreviations (RNAi, WT,
  KO, PCR) bare. Do not re-expand them, and do not define an abbreviation that
  is never used again.
- **Coined terms** keep the authors' quotation marks (Science often uses
  single quotes) at their introduction: we term this 'cascade mapping'.
  Keep the authors' gene, strain and tool names and their capitalization
  exactly as given.

## Rates to match (across all sections)

- **Sentence length**: mean ~25–29 words with a wide spread (SD ~10). Write
  at least one sentence over 35 words per paragraph: two findings joined by
  "and", "whereas" or "although", or a main clause followed by ", indicating
  that …" or ", which …". Put a short flat sentence (under 12 words) next to
  it. Never split a long finding into two balanced sentences.
- **Commas**: ~1.5 per sentence. Sentences carry subordinate clauses,
  appositives and participial tails.
- **Evaluative words** (key, critical, novel, potent, robust, highlight,
  demonstrate, notably): ~4–5 per 1,000 words, so about one per paragraph,
  more often in abstracts and Discussion. Use "demonstrate" and "reveal"
  where authors would. Prose with none of these reads as machine-edited.
- **This/These openers**: about one sentence in seven starts with "This" or
  "These" plus a noun ("These mice …", "This effect …", "These data
  indicate …").
- **Sentence-initial connectives**: "However", "Thus", "Furthermore",
  "Together", "Collectively", "Unfortunately" and "Surprisingly" all occur,
  about one per paragraph. Prefer a sentence-initial "In contrast" over a
  mid-sentence ", by contrast," set off after the subject. Do not chain
  several connectives across consecutive sentences.
- **Hedges**: fewer than machine drafts write (~1 per 1,000 words in
  Results, Methods and introduction). State a mechanism or cause plainly
  when the notes state it ("caused by", "because"). Do not add "likely",
  "may", "so far" or "some of which" to it. Prefer direct modals ("will
  need to", "has been challenging") to softened ones ("will likely require",
  "has so far proven difficult").
- **Semicolons**: ~3 per 1,000 words, and mostly in Methods or inside
  parentheses. Do not join two independent clauses with a semicolon in an
  abstract, introduction or Discussion.
- **Dashes and colons**: about one of each per two or three paragraphs,
  e.g. an en- or em-dash aside or a colon introducing a list. Do not leave
  them out entirely.

## Wording

- **Repeat terms.** Use the same noun and verb for the same thing in
  adjacent sentences ("cultured … cultured", "using … using", the full
  medium name twice). Rotating synonyms (cultured/maintained/grown;
  obtained from/provided by/a gift from) is the commonest tell.
- **Plain lab and field verbs**: cut out, dissolved, amplified, ran,
  blocked, induced. Avoid elevated or nominalized versions: excised,
  resuspended, resolved, labeling achieved by use of, conferring X on Y
  allowed, install, encompass, proven recalcitrant, offer a paradigm,
  bear on, argue against.
- **Compact noun phrases** are normal ("mutant KRAS driven tumors", "kinase
  dead allele", "CDKs 2 and 4"). Do not unpack them into "tumors driven by
  mutant KRAS" or "CDK2 and CDK4", and do not add articles ("the growth of",
  "the fitness of") that a compact phrase does not need.
- **No added framing.** Do not open with a purpose clause ("To account for
  …", "To ask which …") unless the notes give that motivation. Do not add
  clarifying insertions, glosses or "when X and how Y" tails that the
  content does not need.
- **Order** follows the notes. Do not regroup material into a tidier
  sequence (e.g., moving a quality-control step to the end).
- **Tense** is mixed. Authors often report their own findings in the past
  ("we found", "we identified", "deletion reduced …") and use the present
  for what figures show and for general facts. Do not make a paragraph
  uniformly present.
- **Enumerations** in a Discussion list future directions with a repeated
  frame ("would enable …", "would enable …") rather than a different verb
  for each item.

## Section rules

**Abstract**: ~125 words, ~6 sentences of ~22 words, **no citations**,
almost no parentheses. One or two background sentences, kept separate
rather than fused into one long contrast. A gap sentence ("… is highly
limited, owing to …", "remains unclear"). Then "Here, we …" with the comma,
two or three findings sentences ("we demonstrate …", "Furthermore, we show
…"), and a closing significance sentence that may start "Collectively," or
"Together," and use "this work provides …". An appositive gloss after a
name is fine ("X, a soil bacterium resistant to …"); do not turn it into a
separate clause after a semicolon.

**Introduction**: ~140 words, ~6 sentences of ~25 words. This is the most
heavily cited prose: about four citation groups per paragraph, often
several refs per claim, written [6,7] or [13–16] with no spaces. Sentences
open with the subject or with "Although …," and carry ", indicating that
…" tails. Evaluative adverbs ("Unfortunately", "Surprisingly") occur. A gap
is often framed in one sentence: "While X has …, whether Y … is unknown."
The last paragraph ends with "Here, we …" and often (Fig. 1A).

**Results**: ~150 words, ~7 sentences averaging 24 words, with the widest
spread of any section. About five parentheses, mostly figure panels. Open
with "We next …", "We first …" or directly with the finding. Use a purpose
opener ("To test whether …, we …") at most once in two or three
paragraphs, and only for a real question. A closing conclusion ("These
results indicate …", "Thus, …") is optional and plain. Do not attach a
mechanism clause to it ("…, which promotes …"). "In contrast" and "Next"
are common.

**Discussion**: ~145 words, ~6 sentences of ~26 words, almost no
parentheses. This is the most hedged section (~2 hedges per paragraph),
but the hedges sit on real speculation, not on what the data showed. Open
with "Here, we …" or the advance stated plainly. Set results against prior
models with citations. End on an implication or an open question in plain
words.

**Methods (Materials and Methods)**: ~135 words, ~6 sentences of ~24
words. This is the most passive section (~5 passive constructions per
paragraph), with "we" rare. Write one step per sentence in the order
performed, and let short flat steps stand alone ("The DNA was dried and
dissolved in water."). Do not merge the last steps. Catalog numbers,
concentrations, temperatures and software versions go in parentheses, and a
source list may run long with "respectively". Reuse "using", "as described"
and the same medium names without variation. Abbreviations already standard
in the paper (WT, KO, PBS) appear bare. Capitalize genus names (Mycoplasma).
