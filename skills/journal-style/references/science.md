# Science

Measured on Science research articles (training profile: 21 abstracts, 35
introduction, 274 Results, 69 Discussion and 236 Methods paragraphs).
Numbers are per paragraph unless stated. Expert readers compare skill-written
paragraphs with published ones. The commonest tells are text that is too
tidy, too even and too careful. Published Science prose has longer, looser
sentences, more evaluative words, fewer hedges and fewer semicolons than
machine drafts.

## House conventions

- **American spelling**: analyze, behavior, color, center, labeled, modeling,
  signaling, tumor.
- **Serial comma** before "and"/"or" in lists of three or more ("A, B, and C").
- Approximations with "~" (~40%, ~3 kb) or "approximately"; "about" is rare.
- "e.g.," and "i.e.," in parentheses; "for instance" in running text.
- Figure references use **capital panel letters**: (Fig. 2B), (Fig. 2, B and C),
  (Fig. 3, A to D). Supplementary items are lowercase: (fig. S4), (table S2),
  (movie S3). Combine main and supplementary items with a semicolon:
  (Fig. 4A; fig. S7). Put statistics in their own parenthesis next to the
  figure reference rather than merging both into one long parenthetical.
- Numeric ranges in text are usually written with an en dash (10–30%, 2–4 hours).
  "to" appears in figure-panel ranges. Write numbers in the form the notes give
  them; do not convert every range or number to one format.
- Statistics are compact: P < 0.01, (n = 8 mice). Thousands take commas.
- **Abbreviations**: give the full term first and the abbreviation in
  parentheses, at first use in the paper. A paragraph from the middle of
  Results, Discussion or Methods uses established abbreviations (RNAi, WT,
  KO, PCR) bare. Do not re-expand them, and do not define an abbreviation that
  is never used again.
- **Coined terms** keep the authors' quotation marks (Science often uses
  single quotes) at their introduction: we term this 'cascade mapping'.
  Keep the authors' gene, strain and tool names and their capitalization
  exactly as given.

## Rates to match (across all sections)

- **Sentence length**: mean ~25 words, and most sentences run 15–35 words. Very short sentences are occasional, not a fixture of every paragraph. Do not pair a six-word sentence with a 45-word one for contrast.
- **How sentences grow long: by subordination, not by stacking.** Science sentences carry their extra material in a few recurring shapes:
  - a fronted "While …," or "Although …," clause before the main finding (prefer this to a mid-sentence "yet");
  - a nonrestrictive ", which …," clause or an appositive set off by commas ("X, a soil bacterium, …"), with any evaluative adjective before the appositive's noun;
  - a trailing relative clause carrying a proportion (", a third of which …");
  - a participial tail (", indicating …", ", leading to …", ", thereby preventing …") when the content states the consequence;
  - an elliptical contrast ("Wild-type cells responded, whereas mutants did not") instead of repeating the verb and object.
  Do not convert these into coordinated predicates, hyphenated modifiers or a separate sentence. Put a test and its outcome in one sentence, not a question sentence followed by an answer sentence. Keep two facts about the same subject in one sentence joined by "and", rather than starting a new sentence that repeats the subject.
- **Commas**: ~1.5 per sentence. Drafts run low because they drop the set-off clauses above.
- **Evaluative words** (key, critical, novel, potent, robust, highlight, demonstrate, notably): ~4–5 per 1,000 words, so about one per paragraph, more often in abstracts and Discussion. Use "demonstrate" and "reveal" where the authors would. Prose with none of these reads as machine-edited.
- **This/These openers are rare in Science**: about one sentence in twenty. Use one only when the sentence adds new content about something just named ("These mice also …"). Do not use one to recap, and never write "This raises the question …".
- **Sentence-initial connectives**: about one or two per paragraph in the introduction, Results and Discussion, and rare in Methods. Authentic choices: However, Thus, Therefore, Indeed, In contrast, By contrast, Similarly, Furthermore, Together. Two or three across consecutive sentences are house style when each marks a real step (a turn, then supporting evidence, then a consequence). Avoid only chains of additive connectives. Put the connective at the front of the sentence, not after the subject, and write a bare "In contrast," without restating what is compared. Open the second of two parallel examples with "Similarly,". Do not add "Unfortunately" or "Surprisingly" unless the content carries that judgment.
- **Hedges**: fewer than machine drafts write (~1 per 1,000 words in
  Results, Methods and introduction). State a mechanism or cause plainly
  when the notes state it ("caused by", "because"). Do not add "likely",
  "may", "so far" or "some of which" to it. Prefer direct modals ("will
  need to", "has been challenging") to softened ones ("will likely require",
  "has so far proven difficult").
- **Semicolons**: ~3 per 1,000 words, and mostly in Methods or inside
  parentheses. Do not join two independent clauses with a semicolon in an
  abstract, introduction or Discussion.
- **Dashes and colons**: about one of each per two or three paragraphs,
  e.g. an en- or em-dash aside or a colon introducing a list. Do not leave
  them out entirely.

## Wording

- **Repeat terms.** Use the same noun and verb for the same thing in
  adjacent sentences ("cultured … cultured", "using … using", the full
  medium name twice). Rotating synonyms (cultured/maintained/grown;
  obtained from/provided by/a gift from) is the commonest tell.
- **Plain lab and field verbs**: cut out, dissolved, amplified, ran,
  blocked, induced. Avoid elevated or nominalized versions: excised,
  resuspended, resolved, labeling achieved by use of, conferring X on Y
  allowed, install, encompass, proven recalcitrant, offer a paradigm,
  bear on, argue against.
- **Compact noun phrases** are normal ("mutant KRAS driven tumors", "kinase
  dead allele", "CDKs 2 and 4"). Do not unpack them into "tumors driven by
  mutant KRAS" or "CDK2 and CDK4", and do not add articles ("the growth of",
  "the fitness of") that a compact phrase does not need.
- **No added framing.** Do not open with a purpose clause ("To account for
  …", "To ask which …") unless the notes give that motivation. Do not add
  clarifying insertions, glosses or "when X and how Y" tails that the
  content does not need.
- **Order** follows the notes. Do not regroup material into a tidier
  sequence (e.g., moving a quality-control step to the end).
- **Tense** is mixed. Authors often report their own findings in the past
  ("we found", "we identified", "deletion reduced …") and use the present
  for what figures show and for general facts. Do not make a paragraph
  uniformly present.
- **Enumerations** in a Discussion list future directions with a repeated
  frame ("would enable …", "would enable …") rather than a different verb
  for each item.

## Section rules

**Abstract**: ~125 words, ~6 sentences of ~22 words, **no citations**,
almost no parentheses. One or two background sentences, kept separate
rather than fused into one long contrast. A gap sentence ("… is highly
limited, owing to …", "remains unclear"). Then "Here, we …" with the comma,
two or three findings sentences ("we demonstrate …", "Furthermore, we show
…"), and a closing significance sentence that may start "Collectively," or
"Together," and use "this work provides …". An appositive gloss after a
name is fine ("X, a soil bacterium resistant to …"); do not turn it into a
separate clause after a semicolon.

**Introduction**: ~140 words, ~6 sentences of ~25 words. This is the most
heavily cited prose: about four citation groups per paragraph, often
several refs per claim, written [6,7] or [13–16] with no spaces. Sentences
open with the subject or with "Although …," and carry ", indicating that
…" tails. Evaluative adverbs ("Unfortunately", "Surprisingly") occur. A gap
is often framed in one sentence: "While X has …, whether Y … is unknown."
The last paragraph ends with "Here, we …" and often (Fig. 1A).

**Results**: ~150 words, ~6 sentences averaging 24–25 words. About five parentheses, mostly figure panels.
- Open with "We next …", "We first …" or directly with the finding. Use a purpose opener ("To test whether …, we …") at most once in two or three paragraphs, and only for a real question.
- Report each experiment with its outcome in the same sentence. When an argument builds (a contrast, then prior evidence, then the experiment that follows from it), let a short connective open each step.
- A closing conclusion ("These results indicate …", "Thus, …") is optional and plain. Do not attach a mechanism clause to it.

**Discussion**: ~145 words, ~6 sentences of ~26 words, almost no parentheses. This is the most hedged section (~2 hedges per paragraph), but the hedges sit on real speculation, not on what the data showed.
- Open with "Here, we …" or the advance stated plainly. A comparison with related systems can be one long opening sentence carrying the topic, an appositive and a relative clause.
- Frame a gap as a contrast, not an addition: a "While X is known [refs], how Y …" opener or a "but" clause, often with a time word (previously, long, never).
- Put the evaluation of the authors' own result in the subject noun phrase ("The surprising stability of …"), not in a fronted adverb. About one sentence per paragraph may open with an attention marker (Notably, Interestingly, Of note) before a structural or mechanistic observation.
- When the content states a consequence, attach it as a participial tail. Do not write a "so that … can" clause.
- List prospective approaches in one sentence, with every item in the same form (often gerunds) and one hedge for the whole list. Never label items by their position.
- End on an implication or an open question in plain words.

**Methods (Materials and Methods)**: ~135 words, ~5–6 sentences of ~24 words, past tense throughout, including what a reagent, primer or construct does in the protocol.
- **Voice depends on the paragraph.** Bench protocols are mostly passive (~5 passives), with "we" rare. Analysis and computational paragraphs often narrate the authors' decisions in first person with sequencing words ("We binned …", "We next excluded …"), mixed with passives for criteria and thresholds. Do not convert an analysis paragraph wholly to passive.
- **Start with the first action**, with the material or technique as subject. Do not open with a sentence stating what the procedure measured or was for. If the content gives an aim, keep it on the first step's sentence.
- **Keep the content's sentence grouping.** Parallel branches (one dataset processed one way, another a different way) share one long sentence joined by "or" or "and". A step's qualifier goes in a set-off clause or parenthesis after the term it qualifies. A definition or threshold can stand as its own short sentence. Do not regroup the steps into a tidier order.
- **Keep the authors' notation and shorthand.** This covers lab abbreviations, ranges in the form given (dash or "to"), relations given in words or as symbols, list markers such as (i) and (ii), colon-labeled sub-steps inside parentheses, and compact "respectively" pairings. Correct only real errors.
- **Add nothing.** No sequencing adverbs, per-sample clarifications, stressing adverbs, extra steps or numerator-over-denominator restatements that the content lacks.
- Catalog numbers, concentrations, temperatures and software versions go in parentheses. A source list may run long with "respectively". Repeat "using", "as described", the same medium names and the same frame for each reaction without variation. Abbreviations already standard in the paper (WT, KO, PBS) appear bare. Capitalize genus names (Mycoplasma).
