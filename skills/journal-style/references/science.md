# Science

Profiled on Science research articles (21 abstracts, 35 introduction, 274
Results, 69 Discussion, 236 Methods paragraphs). Numbers are per paragraph
unless stated. Blind readers spot skill drafts almost entirely because the
drafts are *smoother* than published text: they merge, reorder, explain,
hedge and normalize. The most useful single instruction is to write the
notes as they stand — one point per sentence, in the order given — and stop.

## The eleven strongest tells (avoid all)

1. Return the paragraph only: no preamble, no comment on the task.
2. Do not fuse two separate points from the notes into one compound clause,
   and do not split a crowded note into two balanced sentences. Long
   sentences should come from crowded content, not from joining.
3. Do not insert rationale or bridging logic. Write the step or the result;
   never "because this is hard at low abundance, we optimized …".
4. Do not add connectives the notes do not imply (then, therefore, likewise,
   similarly, in addition, together with, as well). About one
   sentence-initial connective per paragraph. Prefer plain "However" to a
   balanced "In contrast" unless a genuine two-way comparison is being drawn.
5. Do not reorder: no moving a causal or "due to" clause to the end of its
   sentence, no demoting an abrupt opening fact into a subordinate clause, no
   relocating a pointer to reagents or a supplementary table to a smoother
   place. A short bare pointer sentence may stand first.
6. Do not regularize notation. Keep ranges, unit spacing, hyphens,
   degree signs, cycle shorthand (e.g. bracketed program repeated ×30),
   and spelled-out versus abbreviated units exactly as the notes write them,
   even when the paragraph is internally inconsistent.
7. Do not re-expand an abbreviation already in use in the paper, and do not
   introduce a parenthetical abbreviation you then never use again.
8. Do not vary vocabulary. Repeat the noun, the verb and even the root in
   adjacent sentences ("transfected … transfected"; "enables … enabling").
   Elegant variation is the commonest giveaway.
9. Do not convert a result into an attribution. Write "4.2% of cells carried
   the insertion", not "the assay showed that 4.2% of cells carried it".
10. No trailing ", which …" consequence tails, no passive-infinitive tails
    (", allowing the boundary to be mapped"), no demonstrative bridge
    sentence that restates the previous object before adding to it.
11. No balanced rhetorical pairs: "and conversely", a
    sufficient/failed-to-rescue couplet, "requires no X and imposes no Y",
    antithesis ("not a clock but a switch"), or a concession pivot
    ("A was reported to …, but our data show …").

## Rates to match

- **Semicolons**: none outside Methods and parenthetical lists. Never join
  two independent clauses with one in an abstract, introduction, Results or
  Discussion paragraph, and never use one to tack a reference pointer on.
  (Methods ~3 per 1,000 words.)
- **Voice**: Methods is heavily passive (~38 passive constructions per 1,000
  words; "we" rare). Everywhere else, write first-person active. Target
  "we/our" at ~19 per 1,000 words in abstracts, ~16 in Results, ~12 in
  Discussion, ~6 in introductions. Passive elsewhere runs ~7–11 per 1,000
  words, i.e. one or two constructions per paragraph at most.
- **Commas**: ~1.2–1.5 per sentence (abstract ~1.0). Do not add appositive
  glosses, participial tails or explanatory insertions that raise this.
- **Sentence length**: mean 22–26 words; SD 7–10 (widest in Results and
  Methods, narrowest in abstracts and introductions). Include one sentence
  under 12 words per paragraph, even a bare one ("Both alleles were
  sequenced.").
- **Word length**: mean 5.6 letters. Use the field's full technical term
  rather than a shorter everyday paraphrase, and avoid stock academic
  phrases in place of a specific one.
- **Hedges**: Discussion ~13 per 1,000 words (two per paragraph, on real
  speculation); abstract, introduction and Results ~5 (about one); Methods
  ~0. Never hedge a measured result, and never add "likely", "appears to",
  "may reflect" to a statement the notes make flatly. Where the notes supply
  an unusual or blunt word for an open question, keep it instead of a
  smoother stock phrase.
- **Evaluative words** (key, critical, potent, robust, notably, demonstrate,
  reveal, surprisingly, unfortunately): ~7 per 1,000 words in Discussion,
  ~4.5 in abstracts, ~2.5 in Results, almost none in Methods. One per
  paragraph in most sections; zero reads machine-edited.
- **This/These + noun openers**: about one sentence in ten outside Methods;
  essentially none in Methods.
- **Dashes**: under one per 1,000 words; a dash-set aside or informal
  dash-list from the notes is kept, but do not add one. Colons are
  occasional, mostly before a list.

## House conventions

- American spelling (analyze, behavior, signaling, tumor), except keep the
  authors' own spellings and capitalization of named compounds, gene,
  strain, instrument and reagent names as given.
- Serial comma in lists of three or more. Approximations with "~".
- Figures: (Fig. 2B), (Fig. 2, A and B), (Fig. 3, A to D); supplementary
  lowercase: (fig. S4), (table S2). Keep statistics in a parenthesis of
  their own next to the figure call; P < 0.01, (n = 8 mice).
- Keep compact shorthand from the notes: grouped symbols ("kinases 2 and 4"),
  pre-nominal stacks ("mutant allele driven tumors", "kinase dead mutant").
  Do not expand them into relative clauses, separate symbols, or
  article-laden phrases.
- Coined terms keep the authors' quotation marks at first use.

## Sections

**Abstract** (~124 words, ~6 sentences of ~22 words, no citations, almost no
parentheses): one or two background sentences, left as separate sentences; a
gap sentence ("… remains unknown", "no method exists for …") with plain
causal wording, not an elevated connective; then "Here, we …" and two or
three findings with "we show/we find/we developed". A closing sentence may
state the consequence, but write it as a result or a capability, not as a
claim about the paper; do not open it with a summary adverb plus a statement
of what the work provides.

**Introduction** (~141 words, ~6 sentences of ~25 words, commas ~1.8): the
most densely cited prose, ~4 citation groups per paragraph, written (6, 7)
or (13–16) in the journal's format with several refs per claim. Open with
the subject, or with "Although …,". Frame the gap in one sentence. The final
paragraph states what was done, in the past tense, with the specific
molecular or experimental detail first; it need not begin with a formula.

**Results** (~150 words, ~7 sentences of ~24 words, SD ~10, ~5 parentheses,
mostly panel calls): open with "We next …", "We first …", or the finding.
A purpose opener ("To test whether …, we …") at most once in three
paragraphs, and only when the notes state that question. Report what was
observed in the first person and past tense. A closing "These results
demonstrate/indicate …" sentence is optional; keep it to the claim itself
with no appended mechanism, condition or qualifying clause.

**Discussion** (~143 words, ~6 sentences of ~26 words, almost no
parentheses): state the advance plainly, set it against prior reports with
citations as separate sentences, and use established abbreviations bare.
Hedge the interpretation twice at most. Enumerate future directions with a
repeated frame ("would allow …, would allow …"). End on an open question or
a stated consequence in plain words.

**Methods** (~133 words, ~6 sentences of ~24 words, SD ~9): passive,
one step per sentence in the order performed, "we" rare. Let short flat
steps stand alone and do not merge the closing ones. Catalog numbers,
concentrations, temperatures, volumes and versions go in parentheses or
inline as the notes give them; a long source list may end with
"respectively". Reuse "using" and "as described" rather than varying them.
Bench shorthand stays shorthand; do not rewrite a thermocycler program into
prose or re-sequence a sentence to avoid opening with a numeral. Hedges and
evaluative words: none.
